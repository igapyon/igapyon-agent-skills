# AI Text Naturalness Review

Use this reference to review whether an article, post, README, GitHub text,
documentation, UI text, or short message feels overly generated by AI.

The goal is not to prove authorship. Do not claim that text was generated by
AI. Instead, review which textual features may make readers feel that the text
is AI-generated, over-structured, over-neutral, or detached from the author's
own judgment and experience.

Do not treat structure, neutrality, completeness, or readability as problems by
themselves. These are often signs of good documentation. Treat them as AI-like
signals only when they make the author's purpose, evidence, judgment, or
specific experience hard to see.

## Review Priority

Use this review after safety and respect review.

If safety, respect, privacy, defamation, or human-rights concerns exist, report
those first. Then review AI-text naturalness when it is relevant to the user's
request.

## Scope

Apply this review to:

- articles and blog drafts
- social posts
- README and documentation text
- GitHub PR descriptions and release notes
- product announcements
- UI copy and CLI messages
- short messages before publication or sending

This review is especially useful when the user asks whether the text feels
AI-like, too polished, too generic, too neutral, too structured, or not enough
like the writer's own voice.

## Core Checks

Look for these signals:

- a broad, polished introduction before the author's concrete point appears
- generic framing such as "In modern development", "It is important to", or
  "This article explains" without a specific reason for this text
- repeated balanced structures such as "not only A but also B" or "by doing X,
  we can Y"
- too many safe summary phrases such as "in conclusion", "overall", "in this
  way", "it can be said", or "this is important"
- paragraphs with similar length, rhythm, and sentence structure
- clean bullet lists that feel complete but not situated in actual experience
- abstract nouns such as "efficiency", "quality", "productivity",
  "visibility", "flexibility", or "reliability" without concrete examples
- explanations that are correct but could apply to many projects, products, or
  situations
- excessive neutrality that avoids the author's judgment, doubt, surprise,
  irritation, preference, or decision
- a conclusion that summarizes too neatly without adding a real takeaway
- formal transitions that make the text feel assembled rather than spoken or
  thought through

## Writer Voice Checks

Review whether the text contains enough of the writer's own presence:

- what the writer actually noticed
- what changed their mind
- what was unexpectedly difficult or easy
- what tradeoff they chose
- what they intentionally did not do
- what concrete example made the point visible
- what the reader should care about from the writer's perspective

Do not force casualness. Some writing should remain formal. The issue is not
politeness or structure by itself, but whether the text loses the author's
specific judgment.

## Common AI-Like Patterns

Flag these when they are prominent:

- "This is a very important topic" without a concrete reason
- "There are several benefits" followed by generic benefits
- "By using this, you can improve efficiency" without saying whose work changes
  and how
- "Let's take a look" when the text does not actually feel conversational
- "It is worth noting that" before an obvious point
- "In today's rapidly changing world" or similar broad stage-setting
- a final paragraph that restates the title rather than adding a conclusion
- a full set of sections that looks complete but lacks a sharp center

Do not report these as banned phrases. They are signals. A phrase may be fine
when it fits the writer, audience, and context.

## Rating Guidance

When useful, summarize the degree of AI-like impression:

- Low: mostly natural; only minor generic phrasing or polish.
- Medium: some AI-like structure or generic phrasing is visible, but the
  writer's point still comes through.
- High: the text feels broadly generated, over-neutral, or over-structured; the
  writer's concrete experience and judgment are hard to see.

Avoid fake precision such as percentages unless the user explicitly asks for a
rough scale. If a scale is needed, use plain labels first.

## Review Output

Contribute findings to the [Consolidated Review Report](../../templates/consolidated-review-report.md). Do not emit a standalone `AI Text Naturalness Review` section
unless the user explicitly asks for per-lens reports. Use the canonical fields for
every finding.

When material, add only these lens-specific assessment notes:

```text
Overall: Low / Medium / High
```

If the text does not feel notably AI-like, say so directly and mention the few
minor risks, if any.

## Fix Direction

Prefer small edits that restore specificity and the writer's voice:

- start closer to the concrete trigger, observation, or decision
- replace broad claims with a specific example
- remove one polished summary sentence
- vary sentence length and rhythm
- keep one human hesitation, constraint, or tradeoff
- replace generic benefits with the actual effect on the reader or workflow
- reduce section headings when they make a short text feel over-packaged
- keep useful structure but make the center of the text sharper

Do not rewrite the whole text unless the user asks for revision. In review
mode, point to the phrases or patterns that create the AI-like impression and
suggest targeted repairs.

## Cautions

Do not treat AI-like writing as automatically bad.

Highly structured writing may be appropriate for manuals, release notes,
technical documentation, compliance text, and onboarding material. In those
cases, review whether the structure is useful, not merely whether it feels
generated.

Do not claim to detect AI generation. Review reader impression and textual
signals only.
